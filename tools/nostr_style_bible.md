Working date: 2026-05-31

This archive is not a lexicon. It should read like a sharp, funny, useful nonfiction book about Nostr: clear enough for a first-time reader, alive enough for the lifestyle crowd, and precise enough that a coder does not roll their eyes.

## The House Voice

Write like a brilliant friend at the table who can explain protocol design after midnight without killing the room.

The reader might be a developer, creator, fan, venue operator, Bitcoin person, investor, or Nostr-curious lurker. Assume they are smart. Do not assume they know every NIP, client, relay habit or scene joke.

## What Human Writing Means Here

Human writing has pressure. It wants something from the paragraph. It notices where the reader is standing. It changes pace. It admits friction. It uses concrete examples before abstractions. It lets a sentence be short when the moment needs a punch.

Human writing has taste. It does not say every topic is important in the same gray voice. It knows when a protocol point is nerdy, when a product detail is dangerous, when a cultural detail is funny, and when a claim needs evidence.

Human writing has friction. It says "this is useful, but..." and then explains the trade-off. It avoids pretending every rough edge is a revolution.

## What AI-Writing Usually Gets Wrong

It smooths every topic into the same confident paragraph. It overuses abstract nouns, symmetrical structures, big transitional phrases and vague praise. It explains the map instead of walking the reader through the street.

It repeats internal editorial logic on the public page: "the human question", "the system question", "the reader promise." Those are useful notes for writers, but they sound like scaffolding if left in the article.

It uses phrases that feel expensive but buy nothing: comprehensive, robust, pivotal, nuanced, transformative, unlock, navigate, landscape, ecosystem, delve, tapestry, elevate, leverage, paradigm, seamless, cutting-edge.

## Page Rules

- Start with the lived situation, then open the machinery.
- Keep the technical fact. Change the delivery.
- One paragraph should do one job.
- Vary sentence length. Let some sentences land.
- Use concrete nouns: key, relay, wallet, venue, fan, badge, invoice, signer, client.
- Explain acronyms only when they matter for the next decision.
- Use humor as seasoning, never as a costume.
- Keep the Crays angle practical: creator, fan, venue, payment, status, governance, operator, capital.
- Never write "this page belongs here" unless the reader actually needs a navigation cue.
- Never write about the writing itself when the reader came for the subject.

## Section Rhythm

Each article should feel like a chapter:

1. Hook: why this matters now.
2. Plain version: what it is in normal words.
3. Scene: what a person actually does with it.
4. Under the hood: what the protocol or product has to handle.
5. Trouble: what breaks, misleads or gets overhyped.
6. Crays read: why this matters for creators, fans, venues, operators or future governance.
7. Next move: where the reader goes next.

## Rewrite Checklist

- Did the paragraph keep all factual content?
- Did it remove filler and assistant-like grandstanding?
- Does it sound like a person chose these words?
- Does the first sentence pull the reader forward?
- Does the section contain at least one concrete action, object or consequence?
- Would a coder trust it?
- Would a creator keep reading it?
- Would a venue/operator understand the business consequence?
- Would a Nostr insider feel the page knows the culture without trying too hard?
